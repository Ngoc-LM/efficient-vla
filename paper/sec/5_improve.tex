\section{Improvement: Cheaper, not Less, Re-planning}
\label{sec:improve}

\paragraph{Bottleneck and hypotheses.}
The released checkpoint re-plans at every control step, and a re-plan costs 789\,ms (\cref{tab:latency}); a successful episode (median 100 steps) therefore spends about 79\,s in the policy and 3\,s in the simulator.
Two hypotheses address this cost.
\textbf{H1 (re-plan less):} computation can be shared across control steps without lowering SR, by executing several actions of a chunk, by re-planning only when the predicted gripper command switches, or by reusing the visual prefix of an older image with fresh proprioception.
\textbf{H2 (re-plan more cheaply):} each re-plan can be made cheaper while the policy computes the same function up to precision and discretisation: float16 removes the emulated bfloat16 arithmetic, fewer Euler steps remove discretisation slack (with $S{=}3$ the first action deviates by 0.32 from $S{=}10$, against a sampling spread of 1.37; \cref{sec:analysis}), and a CUDA graph removes the launch overhead of the expert loop.
H2 predicts SR within the non-inferiority margin and, because launch overhead hides arithmetic, a larger graph speed-up once precision is fixed.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
Open-loop execution sets the number of executed actions per chunk to $k$; the event trigger additionally discards the queue when the next queued action switches the gripper sign (then waits 5 steps).
The stale-image variant caches the keys and values of the image and language tokens, recomputes them every 5 steps or at a predicted gripper switch, and appends the current state token at every step.
Float16 casts the VLM and expert weights (the same parameters) and keeps the projections in float32.
For the CUDA graph, the prefix runs eagerly and the $S$-step Euler loop of the expert is captured once per $S$ with static buffers for the noise, the prefix cache and the time steps, then replayed at every re-plan; capture takes 0.25--2.2\,s once, and the replayed actions equal the eager ones exactly (maximum absolute difference 0 in all 8 settings).

\paragraph{Protocol.}
All variants run the 10 LIBERO-Spatial tasks on stored initial states 0--19 (200 episodes; 0--49 for the main comparison) with common random numbers.
Differences in SR are paired: exact McNemar test, Newcombe 95\% confidence interval~\cite{newcombe1998improved}, and Holm adjustment~\cite{holm1979simple} over the comparisons with the released policy.
A variant is non-inferior if the lower confidence bound exceeds $-5$ points, a margin fixed before the runs.
Latencies are measured on isolated calls (\cref{tab:latency}), because the closed-loop runs share each GPU between two processes.

\paragraph{Re-planning less lowers success (H1 rejected).}
The open-loop and event-triggered variants call the policy about five times less often but succeed in 12.0--12.5 fewer episodes per 100 (\cref{tab:sr}); with $k{=}10$ the drop of 9.5 points is not significant after Holm adjustment.
The event trigger removes most gripper chatter (1.2 short gripper segments per 100 steps against 5.0) without recovering success.
The stale-image variant keeps the expert and fresh proprioception at every step and still loses 12.0 points, so proprioception does not substitute for a current image.
This checkpoint was fine-tuned to execute one action per re-plan; the benefit of longer execution horizons reported for a SmolVLA trained from scratch on LIBERO~\cite{sendai2025a2c2} does not transfer to it.

\paragraph{Re-planning more cheaply preserves success (H2).}
Along the path released $\to$ float16 $\to$ $S{=}3$ $\to$ graph, a re-plan takes 789, 616, 245 and 112\,ms: factors of 1.28, 2.51 and 2.19, or $7.05\times$ in total (\cref{tab:latency}).
The linear fits explain the order of these factors.
Without the graph, one Euler step costs 50.5\,ms in bfloat16 and 53.3\,ms in float16, so its cost does not depend on the arithmetic: the expert is launch-bound.
With the graph, the step costs 28.3\,ms in bfloat16 and 9.4\,ms in float16; once launches are removed, the emulated arithmetic becomes visible, and the graph gains $1.38\times$ in bfloat16 but $3.46\times$ in float16 at $S{=}10$.
On 200 paired episodes, bfloat16 with $S{=}3$ succeeds in 79.0\% and float16 with $S{=}3$ in 77.0\%, and neither differs significantly from the released 77.0\%; for float16 the lower confidence bound ($-5.8$) does not yet clear the margin.

\paragraph{Trade-offs and the next bottleneck.}
The float16 gain is specific to GPUs without bfloat16 tensor cores; on newer GPUs the bfloat16 arithmetic is native, and only the step and graph gains would apply (not measured here).
Float16 changes the numerics slightly: at $S{=}10$ and fixed noise, actions differ from bfloat16 by 0.010 on average and 0.049 at most, against a mean action magnitude of 0.17, with no overflow observed.
The graph requires static shapes (batch 1, fixed prefix and chunk length) and one capture per $S$.
The optimised policy is still not real time: 112\,ms per re-plan plus 32\,ms of simulation gives about 7\,Hz, against the 20\,Hz control rate.
The fit $T(S)\approx84+9.4\,S$\,ms shows where the remaining time is: the prefix takes 84\,ms (75\% at $S{=}3$; vision encoder 39\,ms), so even $S{=}1$ would only reach 93\,ms.
Further gains require a cheaper vision-language prefix, which, unlike the levers above, would change the model and need training.
